Get the month right
and the rest follows.

Almost every disappointing Australian trip is a timing problem. This page is the part most guides skip: which months each region actually works, how far things really are, and what will hurt you if you get it wrong.

Kata Tjuta · Christian Mehlführer · CC BY 2.5

Six seasons,
not four.

In Kakadu, Bininj and Mungguy people track six seasons — and the country genuinely does more than four distinct things a year. This is not folklore; it is the more accurate model, and Parks Australia manages the place by it.

Oct – Dec

Gunumeleng

Pre-monsoon storm season

Humidity builds to something physical. Thunderstorms arrive in the afternoon, the first rains green the floodplain, and barramundi move from the waterholes down to the estuaries. Hot, heavy, and dramatic to watch.

Dec – Mar

Gudjewg

Monsoon season

Thunderstorms, flooding and heat together. The floodplain becomes an inland sea, plant and animal life explodes, and most unsealed roads in the park are closed. Spectacular from the air; largely inaccessible from the ground.

April

Banggerreng

"Knock 'em down" storm season

The rain stops and clear skies return, but violent winds flatten the spear grass — which is what the name describes. Waterfalls are at full volume and the country is at its greenest. A short, underrated window.

May – mid Jun

Yegge

Cooler, still humid

Mornings turn misty, the humidity drops, and traditional burning begins — cool, patchy fires lit to clean up the country and encourage new growth. Roads reopen. Arguably the best month in the Top End.

Mid Jun – Aug

Wurrgeng

Cold weather time

"Cold" means nights around 17 °C and days around 30 °C with almost no humidity. Creeks stop flowing, the floodplain dries and wildlife concentrates on the remaining water. This is peak visitor season for good reason.

Aug – Oct

Gurrung

Hot dry weather

Hot, dry and still. The billabongs shrink to their smallest and the concentration of birds and crocodiles is extraordinary. White-breasted woodswallows arrive; the build-up to the next Gunumeleng begins.

Season names and timings follow the calendar published by the Bininj and Mungguy Traditional Owners of Kakadu with Parks Australia. Other nations across the continent keep their own calendars — the Noongar of the south-west track six, the D'harawal of Sydney track six, and none of them map onto European seasons.

Travelling on Country.

Around half of the Northern Territory and large parts of Western Australia and South Australia are Aboriginal-owned land. Much of what makes this continent worth crossing sits on it.

Ubirr · Dietmar Rabich · CC BY-SA 4.0

How to travel respectfully on Aboriginal land

Permits are real
Entering Aboriginal land generally requires a permit, arranged in advance and sometimes weeks ahead. Arnhem Land goes through the Northern Land Council; central Australia through the Central Land Council; Western Australia through the Department of Planning, Lands and Heritage. A licensed tour operator handles this for you, which is often the simplest route.
"No photography" means no photography
Certain sites at Uluru and elsewhere are restricted under customary law — some may only be seen by men, some only by women, some only by initiated people. The signs are not a suggestion or a copyright notice. Photographing them and posting the image causes real harm to people who are responsible for that place.
Take nothing
Removing rocks, artefacts, or sand is illegal and, at Uluru, so common that the park receives returned stones by post most weeks — often with letters from people describing the bad luck that followed. Leave it where it is.
Pay for the guiding
Aboriginal-owned tourism is one of the few economies that works in remote communities. Choosing a community-owned operator over a franchise is the single most useful thing a visitor does, and the tour is almost always better.
Ask before photographing people
Always, everywhere. In some communities images of people who have died are not shown, which is why you will see warnings before archival footage on Australian television.
Language matters, a little
A Welcome to Country can only be given by a Traditional Owner of that place. An Acknowledgement of Country can be made by anyone. Using traditional place names — Mparntwe, Ikara, Wadjemup, larapuna — is easy, correct, and appreciated.

It is further
than you think.

The single most common planning error is treating Australia like a country rather than a landmass. These are real numbers, and the driving times assume nothing goes wrong.

Sydney → Perth
3,290 km · 5 hours by air. Roughly the distance from London to Cairo, and there is no realistic land alternative for a short trip. Perth is closer to Jakarta than it is to Sydney.
Adelaide → Darwin
3,020 km · three to four days driving. The Stuart Highway, sealed the whole way, through Coober Pedy and Alice Springs. A genuinely great drive, but it is not a transfer — it is the holiday.
Alice Springs → Uluru
450 km · 4 hours 30. People routinely assume these are near each other. They are not, and there is one roadhouse worth stopping at in between.
Broome → Kununurra
1,044 km sealed, or 660 km on the Gibb River Road. The Gibb is unsealed, needs a high-clearance 4WD, and takes five to seven days properly. It closes in the Wet.
Fuel and water
Stretches of 300 km between roadhouses are normal and some are longer. Fill up whenever you can rather than whenever you need to. Carry at least four to five litres of water per person per day, plus a reserve in the vehicle you do not touch.
Road trains
Up to 53.5 metres long and four trailers. Overtaking one needs about a kilometre of clear road; if you are not certain, do not start. When one approaches on an unsealed road, slow down and pull well left — the stones it throws break windscreens.
Do not drive at night
Kangaroos, cattle and camels move at dusk and dawn and are effectively invisible until they are on the road. Outside towns, plan to be parked before dark. This is the most-ignored piece of advice in Australian travel and the one most likely to end a trip.
There is no phone signal
Coverage vanishes within twenty minutes of most towns and does not return for hundreds of kilometres. Carry a satellite messenger or a personal locator beacon, tell someone your route, and if you break down, stay with the vehicle — it is far easier to find than you are.

What will
actually hurt you.

Not spiders. Australia's reputation is wildly miscalibrated: the real risks are heat, water, sun and driving, in roughly that order.

Heat
The Red Centre passes 40 °C routinely in summer and NT parks close walking tracks from 9 am when the forecast exceeds 36 °C. Walk at dawn, rest in the middle of the day, and treat that closure rule as a floor rather than a ceiling.
Saltwater crocodiles
Present in every waterway across the tropical north — including freshwater rivers hundreds of kilometres inland, and occasionally on beaches. Do not swim, wade, clean fish at the water's edge, or camp within fifty metres of the bank. Obey the signs even where the water looks empty; that is precisely how it looks.
Marine stingers
Box jellyfish and Irukandji are present in tropical waters roughly November to May. Swim inside stinger nets or wear a full lycra suit. Irukandji are the size of a fingernail and pass straight through a net.
Sun
UV index regularly hits 12 or above, and the burn time in the north can be under ten minutes. Australians are taught "slip, slop, slap, seek, slide" as children for a reason — the country has among the highest melanoma rates in the world.
Surf
Rips cause more deaths than every animal combined. Swim between the red and yellow flags, which mark the patrolled area. If you are caught in a rip, do not swim against it — float, raise an arm, and let it take you out until it releases.
Snakes and spiders
Real, venomous, and almost never a problem. Deaths average around two a year nationally, versus roughly 1,200 on the roads. Wear boots in long grass, do not put your hands where you cannot see, and carry a compression bandage in remote country.
Bushfire
On Catastrophic fire danger days, leaving early is the only reliable plan. Check the relevant state fire service each morning in summer, and never enter a national park on a Total Fire Ban day without checking closures first.
